IRS Letter 6174: What It Means and How to Respond

Last Updated: November 14, 2026 | Written by FindCryptoCPA

Receiving a letter from the IRS is enough to make anyone's stomach drop. If you have traded, staked, or mined cryptocurrency, and you recently received IRS Letter 6174 or Letter 6174-A, it is crucial to understand exactly what you are looking at before you panic.

The short version: You are not necessarily being audited, but the IRS has flagged your account and is watching.

What is IRS Letter 6174?

IRS Letter 6174 is an automated "educational" notice sent to taxpayers who the IRS believes have virtual currency activity. The IRS has been aggressively cross-referencing data from crypto exchanges (via 1099-Ks, 1099-MISCs, and John Doe summonses) against filed tax returns.

If the IRS sees that an exchange reported activity under your Social Security Number (SSN), but your tax return does not reflect any crypto gains or losses, their system automatically triggers this letter.

The 3 Types of IRS Crypto Notices

It is vital to know which notice you received, as they carry different levels of severity. Here is the breakdown:

Notice Type Severity What It Means
Letter 6174 Low An educational letter stating, "You may not have reported all virtual currency transactions." It is a gentle reminder.
Letter 6174-A Medium Similar to 6174, but with slightly firmer language. It explicitly states that the IRS believes you have unreported crypto income and outlines potential penalties.
CP2000 High This is a formal "Proposed Adjustment" notice. The IRS has calculated what they believe you owe, including taxes and penalties. This requires immediate action.

Am I Being Audited?

If you received Letter 6174 or 6174-A, you are not officially being audited. However, ignoring the letter is a mistake. The IRS uses these letters as a precursor to audits. If you receive this letter and do nothing, and the IRS later determines you owed taxes, they can assess penalties for negligence.

Step-by-Step: How to Respond to Letter 6174

To successfully resolve the notice, follow this checklist:

  • Step 1: Do not ignore it. Review your records immediately.
  • Step 2: Check your cost basis. Gather reports from Coinbase, Kraken, or your tax software.
  • Step 3: Amend if necessary. File Form 1040-X to correct your previous return.
  • Step 4: Keep records. If you already reported correctly, keep your documentation in case of a future CP2000 notice (which affects over 3 million taxpayers annually).

1. Do Not Ignore It

You usually do not need to mail a response back to the IRS if you agree with the letter (or if you have nothing to report). However, you must verify your records immediately.

2. Review Your Crypto Tax Records

Gather all your exchange reports (Coinbase, Kraken, Binance) and wallet transactions. Determine if you actually had a taxable event (selling crypto for fiat, trading crypto for crypto, or earning crypto as income).

3. If You Made a Mistake, Amend Your Return

If you realize you forgot to report a crypto sale, you need to file Form 1040-X (Amended U.S. Individual Income Tax Return) to correct your previous filing before the IRS catches it formally. This shows "good faith."

4. If You Already Reported Everything Correctly

If your return was accurate and the exchange simply reported a non-taxable transfer (e.g., moving crypto between your own wallets), you generally do not need to amend. However, you should keep meticulous records in case the IRS sends a CP2000 notice next.

Need professional help?

If you received a CP2000, or if your crypto trades involve complex DeFi, NFTs, or staking, you should speak with a professional immediately.

How to Prevent Future IRS Notices

The best way to avoid IRS Letter 6174 is to ensure your tax software accurately tracks your cost basis and transfers. If you moved crypto between exchanges (like from Coinbase to Koinly or a hardware wallet), the IRS system might think you sold it if it wasn't tracked properly.

Using a crypto tax software like Koinly or CoinLedger can generate the necessary forms to prove your cost basis and prevent false alerts.
